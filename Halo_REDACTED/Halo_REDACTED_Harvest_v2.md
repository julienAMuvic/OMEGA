# Halo: REDACTED — Harvest

Sep 25, 2026 · @Julien Appleby-Millette · Draft 2

Investment proposal to the Activision greenlight committee and Microsoft leadership.

## Executive summary

Halo: REDACTED makes Halo a two-pillar franchise. It is a premium co-op war game set in humanity's first war with the Covenant, and it ships ahead of the next Master Chief epic: it earns on every platform, returns Halo to its roots as a military industrial fantasy game, and gives the Chief's return a proven foundation and a rested audience. We ask for $34M to run 18 months of preproduction at Core scale. Preproduction ends in a decision on a $161M production, which brings development through launch to $195M.

### The opportunity

- **The category is large and paying out.** Co-op games earned over $8B on Steam in 2025, up 9% ([Alinea Analytics](https://alineaanalytics.substack.com/p/helldivers-2-sells-20m-copies)). Helldivers 2 has sold 20M+ copies for over $700M; ARC Raiders has sold 16.3M ([Nexon](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260813331960/en/Nexon-Releases-Earnings-for-Second-Quarter-2026)). Both are $40 games built for squads.
- **Halo is at a turning point.** Activision now steers the franchise ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/activision-taking-over-halo-franchise-and-what-remains-of-halo-studios-will-now-be-a-support-team-2000736427)), Halo now ships on PlayStation, Xbox and PC, and Xbox content and services revenue fell 10% in the June 2026 quarter ([Microsoft](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/investor/earnings/fy-2026-q4/press-release-webcast)). Halo needs a new engine of growth.
- **The Master Chief line needs rest.** The Paramount+ series was cancelled after two seasons ([VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/paramount-has-cancelled-its-halo-show-after-2-seasons/)) and left younger players meeting the Chief as a meme; Halo Infinite killed Cortana off-screen ([Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/gaming/halo-infinite-ending)). A Chief-free Halo has sold at franchise scale before: Halo 3: ODST sold 2.5M copies in its first two weeks ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/halo-3-odst-drops-25-million-halo-reach-confirmed-as-prequel/1100-6232258/)). REDACTED opens a new front without touching the Chief or Cortana.

### The product

Players fight the war the official record left out: the five-year campaign for the colony world Harvest that cost the UNSC more than half its navy's fighting strength, told through the archive of the Office of Naval Intelligence (ONI). The war runs across seven eras, from first contact to the planet's final glassing, at three scales: four-player Strike Ops, Battlefronts of up to 64 players and hundreds-strong Grand Theaters from the ground to low orbit. Under the shooter runs a live war economy directed by AI orchestrators, the same military-logistics design Halo began with in 1997 as a strategy game ([Halopedia](https://www.halopedia.org/Development_of_Halo:_Combat_Evolved)). Forge, Halo's player-creation tool, ships at launch, and the best community missions enter the live war.

### Why it wins

REDACTED is built on the pattern shared by the category's hits: squad play, one tight core loop, a premium price, a strong identity and story-driven seasons. It adds two advantages neither Helldivers 2 nor ARC Raiders launched with: a globally known franchise and Activision's marketing reach, on all three platforms from day one. It draws on five teams only Activision holds together: Call of Duty's gunplay, Warzone's scale, Zombies' co-op design, World's Edge's strategy simulation and Rare's shared-world experience.

The program cost counts all five live years, not only the first, and dates every dollar to launch at 10%. On those terms REDACTED is a right-tailed bet: the Base case of 8M copies does not pay back (0.77×); a breakout at the average of the category's three hits (15.4M copies) returns 1.7×, and a Helldivers-scale result returns 2.0×. Preproduction is priced as the option on that tail, and its gates are built to separate breakouts from the rest before production money moves.

**Spend per owner matters as much as copies.** At Base-case in-game spend ($8 per owner in year one, 20% expansion attach) the program pays back at 11.8M copies; at Target-case spend ($15, 30%) it pays back at 8.4M. Procurement Contracts, theater expansions and the war's seasonal pull are therefore first-order design targets, sized in preproduction.

**The spend builds Halo's next production pipeline.** REDACTED brings Halo's vehicles, weapons, environments and engine work into Activision's workflows, and the next Master Chief game inherits them. About $29M of development value, 15% of the budget, carries straight into future Halo titles.

**AI agents build and test it for less.** The war economy is itself a simulation run by AI agents, so the same agents playtest the game, balance its economy before players arrive and speed the move of existing Halo assets into Activision's tools. The budget takes $12M of savings in the conservative scenario and up to $31M in the target scenario; Gate 2 measures the realized saving before production is budgeted on it. The program also gives Microsoft a flagship showcase for agent-assisted production.

### The upside

Harvest is the product; the REDACTED platform is the upside. Once the live war holds a population large enough to make new theater expansions profitable, the same engine and systems extend to other eras of Halo's history: Halo Rings, Reach before its fall and the Insurrection, and other settings. None of that upside is in the numbers above.

### The ask

Approve $34M for 18 months of Core-scale preproduction, released in three tranches against gates. Preproduction delivers a playable vertical slice, a confirmed production budget, a staffing plan and a demand model for copies and spend per owner. On meeting its targets, the program moves into a 30-month production for launch roughly four years from approval.

## The vision

**Halo began as a strategy game about an army at war. REDACTED takes it back there.** Bungie started Halo in 1997 as a real-time strategy game on its Myth engine, with Warthogs and Marines deployed across open battlefields. By its public reveal at Macworld in July 1999 it had become a third-person action game, and after Microsoft bought Bungie it shipped in 2001 as a first-person shooter ([Halopedia](https://www.halopedia.org/Development_of_Halo:_Combat_Evolved)). Halo's army still feels like a real military because it was designed as a force to command before it became an arsenal to carry. REDACTED puts the strategy game back under the shooter: a live war economy that every player fights inside.

**The war ONI buried.** Harvest is where humanity met the Covenant. The colony went dark in 2525, Preston Cole's fleet barely won back orbit in 2526, and a five-year ground and orbital campaign ran until early 2531; the Covenant returned in 2534 to glass the planet for good. Canon puts the campaign's cost at more than half of the UNSC Navy's fighting strength ([Halopedia](https://www.halopedia.org/Harvest_campaign)) and never shows where that strength went. It records an ONI prowler watching the frigate *Prophecy* die without helping, and an unidentified Spartan-II killed on Harvest. REDACTED shows the rest: the unreported battlegroups, prototype weapons and Spartan-II operations that the Office of Naval Intelligence (ONI) erased.

**Three tiers of truth.** The game is presented as ONI's Harvest archive, and every piece of it carries a clearance tier. *Verified* is canon: its events, ships and people. *Public Record* is ONI propaganda: inflated victories and recruiting broadcasts. *Redacted* is what players live: secret battlegroups, prototypes and extra Spartan-II operations. When something is fun but breaks canon, ONI classifies it and play continues. Every invented asset is destroyed by 2534, which is why later canon never mentions it. The frame lets the studio invent freely without breaking the franchise's history.

**One war, played at every scale.**

&#91;embedded content: The war simulation · 3 AI orchestrators, 1 economy, 3 play scales, Forge feeding the mission pool\]

Three AI orchestrators run the war. FLEETCOM runs UNSC logistics, Covenant command lands forces, digs for the relic and glasses cities, and ONI Section III scuttles prototypes and covers tracks, sometimes against FLEETCOM. Together they read a simulated supply network, find where it is under stress and publish missions that answer those problems at the scale each one needs. Results write back: a convoy saved in a Strike Op raises supply in a sector, which shapes the Battlefront fought there, which moves territory in the Grand Theater. AI troops fill every battle, so no battle waits for a full server, and a problem nobody takes gets worse on its own.

**Seven eras, each a live war.** The story campaign walks through seven eras in order. Once unlocked, each era also runs as its own live war theater with its own arsenal, enemies and canon anchors. All eras reset together each season, and each era's result seeds the next, so a community's war carries its map, its losses and its glass forward from Contact to Ashfall over about a year.

| Era | Years | What players fight through |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I · Contact | 2525 | The colony falls; militia evacuation |
| II · Retribution | 2525–26 | Cole's fleet wins back orbit |
| III · Liberation | 2526–27 | Ground retaken; war industry built |
| IV · Commitment | 2527–29 | Peak investment and prototypes |
| V · Glassfall | 2529–30 | Mass glassing; fighting retreat |
| VI · Relic | 2530–31 | *Spirit of Fire* arrives; the relic is seized |
| VII · Ashfall | 2531–34 | Final glassing; the archive burns |

**Fun over balance; canon sets the anchors, players shape everything between.** UNSC players stay overpowered at every tier, and enemies never become bullet sponges. Difficulty comes from distance to support, enemy density and objective complexity, so a lost deep mission feels like "we spent the orbital strike too early," never "the enemy was too strong." Death is expected, and drop pods make it a spectacle. Players write the tactical ledger with no limits; the story writes the strategic one. Every player victory raises FLEETCOM's confidence, which sends more ships and industry to Harvest, which draws more Covenant forces, which makes the collapse bigger (Appendix E). The community lives through the same false confidence that doomed the real campaign.

**War states are the content engine.** Helldivers 2 already proves the model: its Game Master chooses the Major Orders and where enemies attack, while "the war fought and liberation of planets — that's all you" ([Windows Central](https://www.windowscentral.com/gaming/helldivers-2-director-explains-the-limits-of-joel-the-game-masters-power)). Its major turns are set, and players meet them as they arrive. REDACTED works the same way. Canon fixes where Harvest's war ends; the orchestrators decide when and how each turn lands; players decide everything between: which cities stand, which supply lines hold, which prototypes survive and how much FLEETCOM commits. Those war states carry into the next era and change its maps, arsenals, enemies and missions, so no two lineages play alike. Arrowhead's CEO has said he would "build automation on the gamemaster" to keep Helldivers 2's war running indefinitely ([GamesRadar+](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/third-person-shooter/helldivers-2-ceo-wants-the-galactic-war-to-last-forever-even-if-arrowhead-has-to-sunset-the-game-in-the-far-future-and-the-devs-have-to-make-an-ai-game-master/)). REDACTED starts there.

**Progression grows wide, not tall.** Veterans earn more options, not bigger numbers, so a new player can answer any call for help. Core weapons and vehicles unlock per era; Procurement Contracts from canon manufacturers work like Warbonds and never expire; ONI Black Budget prototypes unlock through field use and clearance and are never for sale. The catalogues tell the story too: contracts multiply through the buildup, and in Glassfall every one is stamped "Procurement terminated."

**PvP stays small and dramatic.** The only way to play the Covenant is as an invader: a commander who drops a small AI lance into a hard mission, limited per operation. The invaded squad can call a quick reaction force, and other players answer by Pelican or ODST drop pod. The UNSC is favored to win, and the rescue becomes the story players tell.

**Players build the war too.** Forge ships at launch with map, mission and scenario tools built on the studio's own toolset. Approved community missions are tagged by the problem they solve, and when the war hits that problem the orchestrators can deploy them. Creators are credited in the archive, and Wargames hosts anything-goes custom games, PvP included, outside canon.

**It holds up when players leave and expands when they come back.** Most live-service games have one minimum population, and below it they close. REDACTED has five operating modes, each needing fewer players and costing less than the one above:

The live war runs in the highest mode its own revenue pays for, so live cost falls with the population, and the game never has to be switched off. That also answers the Stop Killing Games movement, whose European Citizens' Initiative drew a formal reply from the European Commission in June 2026 ([European Commission](https://citizens-initiative.europa.eu/news/european-commission-replies-stop-destroying-videogames-initiative-2026-06-16_en)).

## The franchise platform

**Harvest is the product; the platform is the upside.** Core scale launches Harvest with Eras I–IV, one Grand Theater region, and Map and Mission Forge. Eras V–VII arrive in the first live year and complete the war. From year two, a paid theater expansion each year opens new ground at $20.

&#91;embedded content: Platform path · Harvest's eras and expansions, the self-funding gate, new wars, and pipeline reuse for the Master Chief line\]

**The win condition is a live war that pays for its own growth.** In year t the live war runs in mode m when its owners' spending covers that mode's cost:

`Ñ_t · (s_t + a · p · [t ≥ 2]) · f  ≥  T_m + κ_m · k · A_t`

Here Ñ\_t is effective owners (buyers plus Game Pass players weighted at half an owner), s\_t = s · (1 − d)^(t−1) is gross in-game spend per owner in year t, a is the expansion attach rate, p is the $20 expansion price and f is the net share after platform fees. On the cost side, T\_m is the mode's live team cost ($33M in Mode 1 for 180 people), k is about $4.10 of server cost per active owner-year, κ\_m is the mode's server factor, and A\_t = Ñ\_t · (1 − d)^(t−1) is the active population. The Base case's first live year costs $51M, $33M in team and $18M in servers.

On Base-case terms, the first live year runs at Mode 1 on a planned $22M loss while Eras V–VII ship. From year two, expansions and a larger owner base carry the war, and it funds itself in Mode 2:

| Base case | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 | Year 4 | Year 5 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Owners, including Game Pass players | 4.4M | 6.9M | 7.7M | 8.3M | 8.7M |
| Mode | 1 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| Live revenue ($M) | 29 | 50 | 47 | 43 | 39 |
| Live cost ($M) | 51 | 40 | 37 | 34 | 31 |
| Break-even owners in that mode | 7.8M | 5.1M | 5.7M | 6.1M | 6.4M |

On Target-case terms the war holds Mode 1 in every year. In the Downside and Failure cases it steps to Mode 5 after year one: live cost over five years falls to $41–48M in present value instead of the $145–160M a full war would burn, and the archive covers its own running cost from year two.

Above the Mode 1 line every expansion is profit. Mode 1 runs at full strength, with extra shards added as demand requires, and a full war is the signal to fund the next theater. The same economy, orchestrators, arsenals and Forge tools then carry to other wars in Halo's history: Reach before its fall, the Insurrection, the Halo rings and other settings. Each new war is a content project on a proven platform, not a new game.

**The spend builds the next Master Chief game's pipeline.** Activision has given its new Halo team one goal: "make the greatest Halo game ever" ([GamesRadar+](https://www.gamesradar.com/games/activision-prepares-to-make-the-greatest-halo-game-ever-as-it-takes-control-of-the-franchise-from-halo-studios/)). REDACTED is how that team gets there. It rebuilds Halo's vehicles, weapons, environments and engine work inside Activision's pipeline, and about 15% of its development value, $29M at Core scale, carries straight into the Master Chief game that follows.

**The two games share a foundation, not a team.** The new Halo organization runs as three units: a Halo Foundation group that owns the engine, the asset pipeline and the shared arsenal; the REDACTED lead team; and the Master Chief team. Foundation is staffed first and serves both games, and it is where the $29M reuse credit is built. The Master Chief team begins preproduction on the Foundation pipeline around month 24, when REDACTED is in production, and ships at least a year after REDACTED launches, so it builds on REDACTED's live player data rather than competing with REDACTED for the same people.

**The proving ground returns live answers.** Before the Chief returns, REDACTED shows Microsoft, with its own player data:

- that Activision's team builds Halo combat longtime fans accept;
- that a new Halo game sells on PlayStation and Steam as well as Xbox;
- which eras, vehicles, weapons and enemies players spend their time with;
- that players come back to Halo while the Chief rests.

If the conservative saving is not realized, the Base case falls from 0.77× to 0.73×, and the production budget absorbs it within Gate 3's 10% band.

**Four years from approval to launch, and every production dollar follows proof.** Preproduction runs 18 months at Core scale and ends in a production decision. Production runs 30 months to a simultaneous launch on PC, PlayStation 5 and Xbox. The first live year completes Harvest's war.

**Preproduction builds the riskiest things first.** Each gate releases the next tranche of the $34M only when its criteria are met.

| Gate | Month | What it proves | Pass criteria | Tranche released |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gate 1: Core loop | 6 | Strike Ops generated by a live war economy are fun to replay | Orchestrator v1 publishes Strike Ops in Era I and Era V; 8 of 10 external playtest squads choose to replay | $11M for months 6–12 |

The first $7M funds months 0–6 on approval.

**Preproduction answers eight questions,** each feeding a gate:

1. **Outcomes:** the full comparable-launch record, extended beyond the 14 titles in the appendix, and the traits that separated hits from failures. Feeds Gate 3.
2. **Demand by platform:** who buys, on which store, and how strongly Master Chief fatigue shapes interest in a Chief-free Halo. Feeds Gate 3.
3. **Spend per owner:** willingness to pay for Procurement Contracts and theater expansions, and Game Pass uptake. Feeds Gate 3.
4. **War-state variance:** how far each era's outcome changes the maps, arsenals, enemies and missions of the next, across thousands of agent-simulated lineages and the playtest lineages. Feeds Gate 2.
5. **Population:** which operating mode the game should launch in. Feeds Gate 3.
6. **Budget and staffing:** the bottom-up production budget and studio plan, including the Foundation group shared with the Master Chief team. Feeds Gate 3.
7. **Grand Theater cost:** server cost per player-hour at full scale. Feeds Gate 2.
8. **Portfolio overlap:** how much of REDACTED's revenue is new to Activision. Feeds Gate 3.

**The team scales with the gates.** Preproduction opens with a lead team of about 40 from Activision's new Halo organization and grows to about 140 by month 18. Production follows Call of Duty's lead-and-support model: the lead team owns the sandbox, the orchestrators and Grand Theater technology, Foundation owns the engine and asset pipeline, and support studios each own era theaters, averaging 260 people and peaking at about 330. Halo Studios reviews canon throughout, World's Edge advises on the war economy, and Rare advises on the shared world and invasions.

**Agents are in the pipeline from month one.** The orchestrators are AI agents, so the same simulation plays thousands of wars overnight to tune the economy, difficulty bands and mission mix before a human playtest. Human playtests then focus on what agents cannot judge: feel, fun and story.

## Appendix

### A. Budget model

All budget and return figures come from the REDACTED budget model (`redacted_model.py`, which reproduces the draft workbook's revenue figures exactly and adds the program-cost layers below). Costs are built from headcount by phase:

- **Labor:** a blended $199.5K per person-year, from $240K at the lead studio, $180K at support studios and $90K for outsourcing, split 55/30/15. The lead-studio rate is derived from Spider-Man 2's roughly $300M cost and 380 peak staff ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/what-hacked-files-tell-us-about-the-studio-behind-spide-1851115233)).
- **Non-labor costs:** 15% of labor, for tools, middleware, motion capture, hardware and licensing.
- **Contingency:** 15%. Spider-Man 2 overran its $270M budget by about 11%.
- **Marketing:** 60% of development, below Raph Koster's AAA benchmark of 75–100% ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/the-cost-of-games)) because Activision and Xbox own major channels. Spent 15% in months 24–36, 35% in months 36–48 and 50% in the launch year.
- **Phases:** 18 months of preproduction (tranches of $7M, $11M and $16M over months 0–6, 6–12 and 12–18), 30 months of production spent evenly over months 18–48, and live operations from month 48.
- **Live operations:** a team cost T\_m of $183K per live head (the draft's $33M for 180 people) at the mode's headcount, plus servers at about $4.10 per active owner-year (the draft's $18M at 4.4M Base-case active owners in year one), scaled by the mode's server factor κ\_m. The Grand Theater cost study replaces the server rate with cost per player-hour.
- **Franchise reuse credit:** 15% of development, carried into later Halo titles.

**Revenue** is valued over five years at launch:

`PV_rev = Σ (t = 1…5) [ Σ_q c_(t,q) · P · (1 − φ_q) + Ñ_t · s · (1 − d)^(t−1) · f + Ñ_t · a · p · f · [t ≥ 2] · [m_t ≤ 4] ] ÷ (1 + r)^(t−1)`

Here c\_(t,q) is copies sold in year t on platform q (55%, 20%, 12%, 8% and 5% of lifetime copies, split 50/25/25 across Steam, PlayStation and Xbox, with Xbox copies from year two halved by Game Pass), P is the $35 realized price, φ\_q is the platform fee (Valve's tiered share on the game's lifetime Steam revenue, 30% on PlayStation, and 0% or 30% on Xbox by view), f = 1 − Σ\_q w\_q φ\_q is the blended net share, Ñ\_t = O\_t + μ · G\_t is effective owners (cumulative buyers O\_t plus Game Pass players G\_t weighted by μ = 0.5), s is gross in-game spend per owner in year one, d is the 30% annual decline in spend and engagement, a is the expansion attach rate, p is the $20 expansion price, m\_t is the operating mode and r is the 10% discount rate.

**Program cost** is dated to the same point:

`C = Σ_j K_j · (1 + r)^((48 − τ_j) / 12) + Σ (t = 1…5) [ T_(m_t) + κ_(m_t) · k · A_t ] ÷ (1 + r)^(t−1) − R_reuse`

where K\_j is each month's development and marketing spend at month τ\_j from approval, A\_t = Ñ\_t · (1 − d)^(t−1) is the active population, k is the server rate and R\_reuse is the $29M credit.

**Mode ladder:** m\_1 = 1, and for t ≥ 2 the war runs in the highest mode whose cost its live revenue covers:

`m_t = min { m ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4} : T_m + κ_m · k · A_t ≤ L_t(m) },  else m_t = 5`

with L\_t(m) the in-game and expansion revenue of year t in mode m.

**Returns:** `NPV = PV_rev − C` and `multiple = PV_rev ÷ C`. Copies needed for a multiple M solve `PV_rev(N) = M · C(N)`; both sides depend on N, because live and server costs scale with the population.

| Case | Lifetime copies | Spend per owner, year one | Expansion attach | PV revenue | Program cost | NPV |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Failure | 1M | $3 | 5% | $28M | $361M | −$333M |
| Downside | 3M | $4 | 10% | $91M | $368M | −$277M |
| Base | 8M | $8 | 20% | $371M | $485M | −$114M |
| Breakout | 15.4M | $15 | 30% | $953M | $574M | $380M |
| Target | 20M | $15 | 30% | $1,238M | $608M | $630M |

Draft 1 compared that revenue with a cost that included only the first live year and no time value on the cost side. The changes that move the Base case from 1.13× to 0.77×:

### E. The confidence trap

FLEETCOM's commitment grows with the victories it wins, and victories grow with what FLEETCOM commits. Let A\_k be the UNSC force committed in era k, C\_k the Covenant pressure and E\_k the community's play in that era. Success depends on both forces:

`I_k = γ · A_k − δ · C_k + E_k`

and each side escalates in response:

`A_(k+1) = A_k + α · I_k,    C_(k+1) = C_k + β · A_k,    α, β, γ, δ > 0`

In state form x\_(k+1) = M · x\_k + b\_k with x = (A, C):

`M = [ 1 + αγ   −αδ ]
    [   β       1  ]`

Its determinant is 1 + αγ + αβδ > 1, so the escalation grows from era to era. The discriminant of its characteristic polynomial, α²γ² − 4αβδ, sets how it grows:

- **αγ² > 4βδ:** real eigenvalues, the largest λ = 1 + αγ/2 + ½√(α²γ² − 4αβδ) > 1. Commitment and pressure climb together in a steady surge.
- **αγ² < 4βδ:** complex eigenvalues of modulus √(1 + αγ + αβδ) > 1. Offensives and counteroffensives swing back and forth with growing amplitude.

Either way, community play E\_k feeds I\_k, which raises A, which raises C. The loss at Glassfall, the force committed when the authored collapse lands, rises with every era of good play:

`L = A_V,    ∂L / ∂E_k > 0 for every k < V`

The better players do, the larger the loss they live through. α, β, γ and δ are the orchestrators' tuning parameters, and the sign of αγ² − 4βδ chooses between a surge and a see-saw war for each lineage.
