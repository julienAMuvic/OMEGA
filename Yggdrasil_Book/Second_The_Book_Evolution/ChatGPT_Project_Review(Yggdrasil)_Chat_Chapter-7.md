Chapter 7 — Standards as Commons, Not Thrones
=============================================

Why Standards Decide Whether Civilization Scales
------------------------------------------------

Civilization runs on control planes. Dock assignments, power beams, scheduler slots, corridor traffic, and replication lineages are the levers that make everything else predictable. If these levers correlate in failure, entire regions go dark at once. If they remain plural and auditable, outages stay local and recoverable.

Standards decide which path we take. They are not paperwork; they are the spine of the rails: exit and safe passage, rescue first, neutral courts, open corridors and standards, and leashed replication. When standards become thrones—one vendor, one stack, one editor—freedom narrows. When standards are commons—open conformance suites, ≥3 independently developed stacks, rotating editors, and escrowed bug bounties—reliability rises and capture risk falls.

This chapter is a manual for standards editors, infra operators, regulators, and procurement leads who want services that scale without empire. We will keep the tone practical: short steps, real checks, and defaults that fail safe. Where needed, we point to related material (e.g., CUCC Cage in Ch. 3, Sanctions Before Shots in Ch. 8, AHF in Ch. 6).

The Failure Pattern: From “Best Practice” to Monoculture
--------------------------------------------------------

Monoculture rarely arrives by decree. It seeps in through convenience:

*   “Best practice” decks that prefer the incumbent’s test harness.
*   Procurement KPIs that reward short lead times, not outage variance.
*   Vendor-run “interoperability labs” that grade their own homework.
*   Auditors who see green dashboards but not missing logs.

The result: a single scheduler governs the busiest corridor; one docking stack sets the API; one replication lineage tool writes the only accepted signature. Outages correlate because the same bug runs everywhere. Politics drifts because the editor who controls the registry can stall rivals. Operators accept capture in exchange for a promise of speed.

This pattern also erodes the Five Pillars. Birth/Crew Formation slows when training binds to one stack. Language/Codec narrows as proprietary interfaces displace open codecs. Land/Habitat maintenance falls behind when upgrades require synchronized downtime. Skill/Reliability plateaus because teams stop practicing cross-implementation drills. Security (bounded) slips as detection becomes a single sensor and sanctions a single gate.

The fix is structural. You replace incentives and interfaces, not slogans.

Designing Standards as a Commons
--------------------------------

A commons standard is a governance pattern, not just a PDF. It has seven visible parts:

1.  **Open Conformance Suites.** Test cases, fixtures, and sample datasets live in public repos under liberal licenses. Anyone can run them. Anyone can propose new tests via a tracked change process. Passing the suite is binary; waivers are logged with reasons.
2.  **≥3 Independently Developed Implementations.** For Dock/Beam/Sched/Traffic/Replication, the registry only accepts a specification as “active” when three stacks pass the suite on current versions. “Independently developed” means distinct teams, codebases, and governance.
3.  **Rotating Registry Editors.** Editors serve fixed, staggered terms (e.g., 18 months). Selection uses a public eligibility matrix (implementation contributors, operator reps, safety auditors). No consecutive terms from the same organization.
4.  **Grace Windows.** Breaking changes ship with a minimum 12-month adoption window. The registry publishes a “commit–reveal” calendar: commit date for the change set; reveal date for detailed artifacts; adoption window open/close dates.
5.  **Escrowed Bug Bounties.** A dedicated escrow—funded by registry fees and operators—pays reproducibility bounties and severity-scaled bug rewards. Claims require proof in the open suite or a linked reproducer, with redactable steps for security-critical cases.
6.  **Transparent Registries.** Decision logs, ballots, rationales, and dissenting opinions are public. All editor communications on standard work use recorded channels. Meeting minutes include votes and conflicts of interest.
7.  **Anti-Concentration Caps (45/30/25).** No single implementation should exceed 45% share on a control plane; the top two combined should not exceed 75% (45/30); the third must be ≥25% to keep quorum robust. Caps tie to port/slot access and insurance premia.

This skeleton preserves the Rails. Exit and corridors remain real because rival stacks can be swapped in during grace windows. Rescue first holds because green lanes are encoded in suites, not left to vendor discretion. Neutral courts can rule because logs and lineage proofs exist. Open standards exist by design. Replication is leashed: we can prove provenance and throttle lineages across implementations.

Conformance Without Theater
---------------------------

Most “compliance” is theater when tests are friendly and logs are optional. Make conformance adversarial and evidence-rich:

*   **Adversarial Test Design.** Each control plane includes chaos cases: packet loss, clock drift, Byzantine messages, malformed manifests, and degraded sensors. Inject failures at edges most vendors ignore: timestamp rollovers, leap-second handling, flaky NTP, congested backplanes.
*   **Lineage Proofs for Builds.** Every certified binary is reproducibly built from tagged commits with signed dependency manifests. The suite validates deterministic hashes per build profile and stores them in a public ledger.
*   **Reproducibility Bounties.** If an auditor or rival can’t reproduce a published passing result within the suite, they can claim a bounty. The default assumption is “no pass without reproducibility.”
*   **No Pass Without Logs.** Passing means producing portable, queryable logs for each test phase: preconditions, stimuli, outputs, and timing. Logs are retained under a life-cycle policy (e.g., two years) and are admissible in tribunal proceedings (see Ch. 4: Neutral Courts).
*   **Drift Watch.** Conformance drift is measured continuously. Operators run “canary suites” in staging and publish drift deltas weekly. Large deltas trigger grace-window evaluations.
*   **Privacy by Fixture.** Test datasets mask personal or sensitive content by design. Where live-data replay is essential, the suite defines a de-identification pipeline and a retention envelope.

This is “logs before labels” in practice, the AHF principle applied to standards. Labels—badges, vendor claims, marketing—come after the logs demonstrate behavior under stress.

Economics that Reward Diversity
-------------------------------

Standards shape markets by moving the price of risk. If you pay the same premium for monoculture as for plural stacks, you will get monoculture. Rewire the rates:

*   **Insurance Premia Linked to Outage Variance.** Premiums drop for operators whose outage variance is lower during multi-implementation failover drills. Variance is measured across time and across implementations, with uncertainty bands published.
*   **Fee Rebates for Triopoly Conformance.** Registry and port fees rebate 10–20% when an operator runs ≥3 certified stacks in rotation with verified canary tests and publishes drift metrics.
*   **Bonded Tribunal Outcomes.** When disputes land in neutral courts (see Ch. 4), bonds fund remediation based on logs, not brand. Vendors who deviate from suite behavior burn bonds; those who self-disclose and patch within grace windows see bond reductions.
*   **Sanctions-First Governance.** Noncompliant stacks face port/slot schedule denial before force (see Ch. 8: Sanctions Before Shots). Editors do not police behavior; they apply published ladders tied to test results and caps.
*   **Switching Subsidies.** A registry fund underwrites adapters, training, and data migration for operators adding a third implementation. Subsidies are milestone-based (suite pass in staging; canary in production; 90-day stable run).
*   **Price of Secrecy.** Security by obscurity pays a premium. If a vendor insists on closed interfaces or undisclosed cryptographic protocols, their cap share is stricter (e.g., 35% max), and their bonds increase.

Linking money to variance, not slogans, is how we stop theater from winning.

Interop Under Pressure: AHF, CUCC Cage, and Fallback Logistics
--------------------------------------------------------------

Emergencies are where monocultures pretend to shine. They don’t. Correlated failure during a surge is the definition of catastrophe. The right move is plural interop plus constrained command.

*   **AHF Triggers.** Multi-signal triggers (telemetry thresholds, verified incident reports, quorum signals from independent stacks) open green lanes and activate fallback profiles. Evidence packs—logs, hashes, and decision rationales—are published in near real time.
*   **Green Lanes Stay Green.** The suite includes SAR green-lane invariants that all stacks must implement. During protest surges or storms, schedulers prioritize SAR traffic identically, so handover across implementations is seamless.
*   **CUCC Cage for Orchestration.** If central coordination is unavoidable, run it inside the CUCC Cage: two-key authorization (operator + independent safety officer), hard clocks (7/30/90 days), scope box (only reopen the rails), quorum engines, and an explicit re-federalization timetable (see Ch. 3).
*   **Fallback Logistics.** Pre-provisioned adapters and configuration profiles for failover across Dock/Beam/Sched/Traffic/Replication. Canary drills every quarter with public reports. Contracted surge capacity (compute, spectrum, berths) only accessible through the cage with dual keys.
*   **After-Action Feed-Back.** After emergencies, the registry ingests incident packs to add tests to the open suite. Editors must show exactly how new cases simulate the fault and how future implementations will avoid it.

Plural interop keeps the rails open; the cage keeps command temporary, auditable, and narrow.

Procurement That Keeps Us Free
------------------------------

Procurement either builds a commons or buys a throne. Use an explicit, stepwise play that bakes in plurality, caps, and grace.

### A Step-by-Step Procurement Play

1.  **Inventory Control Planes.** List Dock/Beam/Sched/Traffic/Replication functions you procure. Map current implementations, versions, and dependencies. Mark any plane with a single implementation as “red.”
2.  **Adopt the Registry.** Join or help stand up the relevant registry. Confirm rotating editor governance and the existence of an open conformance suite. If no suite exists, fund an initial test skeleton and escrow.
3.  **Write Anti-Concentration into RFPs.** Include 45/30/25 caps by share of control-plane operations. Add “no single emergency scheduler” clauses. State that caps tie to slot access and insurance.
4.  **Mandate ≥3 Certified Stacks.** Require bidders to pass the suite and to interoperate with at least two other stacks in canary. Allow staged compliance: two stacks Day 0, third within 12 months, with switching subsidies.
5.  **Grace Windows and Commit–Reveal.** For any breaking change, require a 12-month adoption window with a commit–reveal schedule. Vendors must publish migration guides and maintain shims for the window duration.
6.  **Bug-Bounty Escrow.** Allocate a fixed percentage (e.g., 1–2% of contract value) to the registry’s escrow. Tie milestone payments to vendors’ responsiveness to escrowed reports.
7.  **Lineage and Logs.** Make reproducible builds and portable logs a pass/fail criterion. Specify log retention, access controls, and audit hooks. Require independent verification of reproducibility.
8.  **Sanctions Ladder.** Define port/slot regimens for nonconformance: warning → reduced share → temporary schedule denial. Tie ladder steps to test results, not discretion.
9.  **Neutral Courts and Bonds.** Require bond-backed dispute resolution with tribunal SLAs (Interim Orders ≤72h; Final Orders ≤30d; see Ch. 4). Make logs admissible under standard formats.
10.  **Training and Crew Formation.** Budget for operator cross-training on all certified stacks. Require a minimum number of dual- and tri-stack certified crew per shift (Five Pillars: Birth/Crew, Skill/Reliability).
11.  **Codec and Adapters.** Favor open codecs for data interchange (Five Pillars: Language/Codec). Fund adapters to prevent lock-in and ease migration.
12.  **Periodic Drills.** Contractually require quarterly failover drills with public summaries and lessons fed back into the registry.

Measuring What Matters
----------------------

If you pick the wrong metrics, vendors will game them. Use measures that resist Goodhart and publish uncertainty:

*   **Outage Variance (OV).** Standard deviation of service unavailability over time and across implementations. Lower OV beats lower mean downtime when measuring resilience. Publish 95% confidence intervals and the sensitivity to outliers.
*   **Interop Latency (IL).** Time to complete a cross-implementation transaction under load. Measure P50/P95/P99. Provide sensitivity to message loss and clock skew.
*   **Conformance Drift (CD).** Weekly delta between running behavior and suite expectations. Express as a percentage of failing or unstable tests out of total relevant tests; include uncertainty bands due to flaky tests.
*   **SAR Uptime (SU).** Availability of search-and-rescue green-lane capacity during peak events. Publish SU as a fraction of time green lanes meet minimum throughput.
*   **Bond Burn Rate (BBR).** Rate at which bonds are used to remedy nonconformance. A rising BBR signals useful enforcement; a flat zero may indicate missing audits.
*   **Reproducibility Rate (RR).** Fraction of passing claims that third parties can reproduce. Target ≥0.95 with confidence intervals.
*   **Lineage Integrity (LI).** Percentage of deployed artifacts with verifiable, signed lineage matching registry hashes.

For each metric, publish uncertainty bands and a sensitivity note: how does the metric change if you exclude the worst day, the worst implementation, or a specific failure mode? This deters dashboard theater and helps neutral courts weigh evidence.

Chapter 7 — Standards as Commons, Not Thrones

Why Standards Decide Whether Civilization Scales
------------------------------------------------

Civilization’s daily reliability rides on a handful of control planes. Docks decide who berths where and when. Beams decide how energy moves across air and vacuum. Schedulers decide which jobs run, preempt, or wait. Traffic controllers decide who passes first and on what corridor. Replication systems decide what gets copied, where, and at what rate. If any one of these planes fails in the same way, at the same time, across an entire network, the failure is no longer technical. It is civilizational.

Standards determine whether those failures correlate. Treat a standard like a throne—one stack, one vendor, one editor—and outages synchronize. Treat a standard like a commons—open conformance suites, ≥3 independently developed stacks, rotating editors, grace windows, escrowed bug bounties, and transparent registries—and the blast radius shrinks. Rights travel with you because interfaces are open and behavior is verifiable. The Rails hold: people can exit through working corridors, rescue lanes stay prioritized, neutral courts can act on evidence, control planes are kept open by multi-implementation standards, and replication remains leashed by signed lineage and rate limits.

This chapter translates that posture into practice for standards editors, infra operators, regulators, and procurement leads. The mechanics are non-negotiable. The prose aims for flow. We keep the tools sharp and show how they fit together under pressure.

The Failure Pattern: From “Best Practice” to Monoculture
--------------------------------------------------------

Monocultures rarely arrive by edict. They slide in on a cushion of convenience.

A vendor’s “best practice” kit becomes the only way to pass acceptance tests. Procurement rewards fastest time to install, not lowest outage variance. Interop labs are hosted by the incumbent and optimized to their harness. Dashboards glow green while logs are thin and unreproducible. Audits confirm paperwork instead of behavior. People tell themselves that one stack is safer “for now,” then never revisit the decision.

The deeper cost is political. Whoever sets the registry controls the pace of change and can stall rivals by shaping the next minor version. Operators who depend on a single scheduler discover they cannot switch without breaking corridor SLAs. Local crews are trained on one stack; new entrants face an expensive learning curve. Interfaces move behind NDAs. The Five Pillars sag: crews become brittle; codecs drift toward proprietary dialects; maintenance windows yoke habitats to one release train; skill plateaus; security narrows to one sensor and one gate.

This pattern is preventable. It persists because we let process substitute for proof. The remedy is structural and enforceable. Make the commons real, and convenience will work for pluralism rather than against it.

Designing Standards as a Commons
--------------------------------

A commons standard is not just a specification document. It is a governance pattern that earns trust under load. The pattern has seven parts that must be visible and auditable.

**Open conformance suites.** Test cases, fixtures, and sample datasets live in public repositories under permissive licenses. Anyone can run them end-to-end. Submissions follow a tracked change process with public rationales for acceptance or denial. The suite includes both sunny-day and hostile cases. Passing is binary. Waivers are rare and logged with scope and expiration.

**≥3 independently developed implementations.** A control plane is considered “active” only when at least three distinct stacks pass the suite on current versions. “Independently developed” means separate codebases, separate core teams, and separate governance. Forks without governance separation do not count.

**Rotating registry editors.** Editors serve staggered, fixed terms. Eligibility balances implementation contributors, operator representatives, and safety auditors. No entity may hold consecutive editorial terms. Voting is recorded with public reasons. Minutes capture dissent.

**Grace windows.** Breaking changes move on a commit–reveal schedule: commit date for the change set; reveal date for detailed artifacts; a minimum 12-month adoption window with required backward-compatibility shims. Operators can plan migrations like maintenance projects, not emergency scrambles.

**Escrowed bug bounties.** A registry escrow—funded by operator dues and vendor fees—pays severity-scaled bounties for reproducible flaws and for failure to reproduce a claimed pass. Sensitive steps can be redacted, but proof must pass through the open suite or its secure variant.

**Transparent registries.** Decision logs, editor ballots, conflict disclosures, and testing rationales are public by default. Editor communications occur on recorded channels. Exceptions (e.g., a security embargo) have clocks, scope, and public summaries once resolved.

**Anti-concentration caps (45/30/25).** In any control plane, the largest implementation may not exceed 45% of operations; the top two together may not exceed 75% (45/30); and the third must be at least 25% to keep quorum meaningful. Caps tie directly to slot access and insurance premia. They are measured and enforced.

This pattern aligns with the Rails. Exit paths stay open because adapters and rival stacks exist and can be enabled within grace windows. Rescue first is encoded as a conformance invariant, not a vendor promise. Neutral courts can adjudicate because the suite yields portable logs and lineage proofs. Open corridors are sustained by multi-implementation standards rather than eroded by single-vendor “interoperability.” Replication remains leashed because lineage is signed across stacks, not asserted by one.

Conformance Without Theater
---------------------------

Compliance becomes theater when tests are friendly, logs are optional, and “passes” can’t be reproduced. Standards that matter behave like adversaries. They try to break you in the same places real incidents do—and they pay people to keep trying.

Start with **adversarial test design.** Beyond happy paths, include chaos cases a real system faces and vendors tend to under-spec: packet loss at threshold; NTP drift and leap-second handling; message reordering; Byzantine inputs; malformed manifests; throttled backplanes; cold-start behavior under partial data. For each control plane, encode the edge conditions that cause human operators to reach for a workaround.

Attach **lineage proofs** to every certified artifact. A passing binary must be reproducibly built from tagged commits with a signed dependency manifest. The suite validates deterministic hashes per build profile and records them in a public ledger. If the hash can’t be reproduced, the pass is not recognized.

Fund **reproducibility bounties.** If a third party cannot reproduce a vendor’s passing result using the suite and the declared environment, they can claim a bounty from escrow. The default assumption flips: “no pass without reproduction.” Vendors who quickly expose, explain, and fix a test fragility keep trust and preserve most of their bond.

Enforce **logs before labels.** No badge, listing, or marketing claim stands without portable logs for each test phase: preconditions, stimuli, outputs, timing. Logs conform to a public schema, with retention policies measured in years, not weeks. This one rule is the most important bridge to neutral courts: the tribunal (see Ch. 4) expects evidence it can parse, not slides.

Measure **conformance drift** continuously. Operators run “canary suites” in staging against every candidate release and publish drift deltas weekly. Deltas above a declared threshold trigger a grace-window discussion. Drift trends better predict pain than mean uptime and force attention on behavior rather than slogans.

Handle **privacy by fixture** rather than permission. Where live data is essential, the suite defines a de-identification pipeline and retention envelope, so tests remain potent without exposing personal information. Removing friction to testing is the best way to keep the suite honest.

Conformance ceases to be theater when a failed pass burns a bond, when a missing log halts a rollout, and when “we shipped on time” is not an acceptable substitute for “we behaved under stress.”

Interop Under Pressure: AHF, the CUCC Cage, and Fallback Logistics
